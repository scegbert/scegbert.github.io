A Quick Introduction

Welcome! I’m Scott, a research scientist at IMRA America’s Boulder Research Lab in Longmont, Colorado. I develop stabilized lasers and precision optical systems, combining fiber and free-space optics, electro-optic modulation, RF electronics, controls, and opto-mechanics. I especially enjoy taking demanding R&D systems, understanding what limits their performance, and turning them into compact hardware that other people can use.

At IMRA, my work centers on compact dual-wavelength Brillouin lasers (DWBLs) and the tools needed to characterize and use them. I have integrated these low-noise optical references with electro-optic and Kerr frequency-division systems spanning microwave, millimeter-wave, and terahertz frequencies. My mechanical engineering background shapes how I approach the whole instrument: optical performance, thermal behavior, packaging, controls, characterization, and troubleshooting.

Before joining IMRA, I earned my PhD in Mechanical Engineering at CU Boulder in Greg Rieker’s Precision Laser Diagnostics Lab, where I developed portable frequency-comb spectrometers and high-temperature molecular absorption databases. Explore my work at IMRA and PhD research for examples, or take a look at my résumé. I’m always happy to connect with people working on interesting optical systems.

My Work at IMRA

Research Scientist · Boulder Research Lab

I develop stabilized optical references and the systems that connect their spectral purity to microwave and millimeter-wave signals. My work spans laser stabilization, optical/RF integration, frequency division, phase-noise metrology, and compact packaging. A central thread has been turning IMRA’s dual-wavelength Brillouin laser (DWBL) from a laboratory-scale reference into portable hardware that collaborators can operate independently.

Why develop millimeter-wave sources?

Electronic millimeter-wave generation often starts with a lower-frequency oscillator and multiplies it upward. Even ideal frequency multiplication raises phase noise by 20log10(N), while amplifiers, mixers, and other stages can add still more noise. Maintaining spectral purity becomes increasingly difficult as the carrier frequency rises.

DWBLs provide a photonic alternative. Two telecom lasers generate correlated Stokes waves in a shared Brillouin fiber cavity, so common-mode cavity and fiber noise is strongly suppressed in their optical frequency difference. A high-speed photodiode can convert that spacing directly into a millimeter-wave signal, or frequency-comb techniques can divide it to lower frequencies. This creates a useful bridge between optical coherence and RF or millimeter-wave signals.

The examples below show several ways our group and collaborators have used that low-noise frequency difference.

Wireless communication

High-capacity wireless links are one application for low-noise photonic millimeter-wave sources. The constellation diagrams below show received signals over a 214-meter link using a 300 GHz carrier.

Molecular frequency references

For applications that require long-term frequency stability, the DWBL spacing can be referenced to a molecular rotational transition. This example compares a free-running DWBL with one stabilized to carbonyl sulfide (OCS).

Resonant tunneling diode injection

A DWBL can also transfer its spectral purity to another oscillator. Injection locking a resonant tunneling diode (RTD) combines the low-noise optical reference with the RTD’s much higher millimeter-wave output power.

Building a portable Brillouin laser

I led the redesign and ruggedization of IMRA’s passively stabilized DWBL, reducing its volume from 570 to 15 liters while improving its performance. The challenge was not simply fitting the same experiment into a smaller box; it was determining which laboratory-scale components were actually necessary and then validating each simplification against the system-level phase noise and stability.

The DWBL produces two narrow optical tones using stimulated Brillouin scattering in a shared fiber cavity. Optical feedback stabilizes the pump diodes, while the common cavity strongly rejects differential environmental noise. Detecting the two tones on a sufficiently fast photodiode produces a millimeter-wave signal at their frequency difference.

The original laboratory system also relied on a large vacuum-insulated fiber spool for thermal isolation. I replaced it with compact nested metal enclosures and a noise-canceling spool design. The smaller package preserved short-term phase-noise performance but became more sensitive to HVAC and diurnal temperature changes. We therefore used an MHz-frequency beat generated with an EO comb to track the DWBL spacing and provide an out-of-loop signal for improved thermal control.

I applied the same measurement-driven simplification to the optical-feedback EOMs. I found that nonoptimal injection power and unfiltered modulation sidebands were limiting technical phase noise. Adding spectral filtering and variable attenuation improved performance enough that the EOM no longer required a high-power RF amplifier, eliminating a large heat source and simplifying the portable system.

Measuring the source’s phase noise

Before optimizing a low-noise source, I want to be sure the measurement actually isolates the quantity I am trying to improve. A conventional two-source phase-noise comparison cannot do that cleanly: the noisier reference at any Fourier frequency can mask an improvement in the device under test.

To address that limitation, our team developed a millimeter-wave cross-correlator. Two independent channels compare one DWBL under test against separate DWBL references, and numerical cross correlation rejects noise that is uncorrelated between the channels. This lets us modify and evaluate one source at a time, which has made design iteration much faster and more quantitative.

Using this measurement, I identified changes that improved both low-offset technical noise and the high-offset noise floor. The compact source now reaches below −160 dBc/Hz at 1 MHz offset on a 260 GHz carrier. Just as importantly, the measurement lets us test whether a component can be simplified or removed without hiding the resulting change behind the noise of a second reference.

Transferring optical stability to new frequencies

300 GHz through Kerr optical frequency division

I integrated a compact 3.3 THz DWBL prototype with a Kerr microresonator oscillator to divide the optical frequency separation down to a 300 GHz carrier. The system achieved 135 attosecond RMS timing jitter integrated from 1 kHz to 1 MHz [5], with the results accepted in Nature Photonics.

Low-noise X-band synthesis

I implemented and characterized an electro-optic frequency-division system that used an EOM comb and feed-forward noise cancellation to transfer the DWBL’s spectral purity to tunable X-band outputs. The system covered 8–16 GHz and improved the phase noise of a commercial microwave synthesizer by up to 30 dB without the servo-bandwidth constraints of a conventional feedback loop [6].

Making the hardware usable beyond our lab

I developed the control GUI and standard operating procedures for the portable DWBL systems so that collaborators could independently operate and troubleshoot the hardware. For me, a system is not finished when it makes a good measurement on my bench; the optical performance, packaging, controls, diagnostics, and documentation all need to work for someone else.

PhD Research at CU Boulder

In Greg Rieker’s Precision Laser Diagnostics Lab at the University of Colorado Boulder, I developed frequency-comb spectroscopy systems for high-speed and high-temperature gas measurements, along with the molecular databases needed to interpret them. The work combined stabilized fiber combs, nonlinear optics, free-space beam delivery, opto-mechanics, FPGA-based phase correction, and scientific software.

The examples below focus on two parts of that work: building a portable mid-infrared dual-comb spectrometer and improving high-temperature H2O spectroscopy for HITRAN.

Building a Portable Mid-IR Dual-Comb Spectrometer

Working closely with Nazanin Hoghooghi, Scott Diddams, and Peter Chang at NIST Boulder, I developed and integrated a GHz-repetition-rate mid-infrared dual-comb spectrometer for high-speed, multi-species absorption measurements. In our first shock-tube experiments, we resolved formaldehyde formation and decomposition into carbon monoxide on microsecond time scales.

After the initial shock-tube measurements, I led the optical and mechanical redesign of the laboratory system into our first portable mid-infrared dual-comb spectrometer, reducing the footprint by more than 80% for rack deployment.

Each channel begins with a stabilized near-infrared fiber comb and amplifier chain. We condition the femtosecond pulses in free space and use intra-pulse difference-frequency generation (IP-DFG) in PPLN to produce broadband 3–5 μm light. The two mid-infrared combs are then combined and delivered to the experiment. My SolidWorks redesign packaged the fiber hardware, nonlinear conversion, and free-space optics into rack-mountable modules while preserving the alignment and stability needed for dual-comb measurements. I also hired, trained, and mentored three interns who supported the redesign and deployment effort.

Improving Spectral Databases

Accurate spectral databases are just as important as the spectrometer itself. I collected 58 high-resolution H2O datasets spanning 300–1300 K to improve near-infrared absorption models used for combustion diagnostics and other high-temperature measurements. Dual-comb spectroscopy was especially useful because each measurement captured nearly 200,000 spectral points over a broad bandwidth while still resolving individual absorption features.

The improved measurements exposed a different bottleneck: analysis. I had increased the measured bandwidth by roughly 3× while reducing noise by about 10×, but the community Fortran fitting workflow was difficult to scale to the dense H2O spectrum. The example below shows the original interface I was working from.

To make that analysis tractable, I developed an object-oriented Python library and GUI around the trusted Fortran solver. The workflow reduced processing time from months to about two weeks and cut database errors from 23% to 1%. I ultimately processed 24,118 parameters across more than 6,000 transitions; the resulting measurements and fits contributed to HITRAN2024. This work shaped how I approach optical systems today: the laser hardware, controls, metrology, and analysis tools all have to be designed around the measurement you ultimately want to make.
